# Composition — Framing, Depth & Visual Balance

## Composition Is Argument

Where you place things in the frame tells the audience what to feel about the relationship between them. Composition is not decoration — it is direction.

---

## Rule of Thirds

Divide the frame into a 3×3 grid. Place key subjects on the intersection points, not at center.

**Why it works:** Off-center subjects create visual tension and dynamic interest. Dead-center framing suggests confrontation, authority, or psychological directness — it is a deliberate choice, not a neutral default.

**Usage:**
- Subject at left intersection, looking right → space ahead of them (hope, possibility)
- Subject at right intersection, looking left → space behind them (retrospection, being followed)
- Horizon on bottom third → sky dominates (freedom, threat, aspiration)
- Horizon on top third → ground dominates (weight, rootedness, entrapment)

**Prompt phrase:** `rule of thirds, subject at [left/right] intersection point, [looking left/right], natural framing tension`

---

## Golden Ratio / Spiral

The Fibonacci spiral places the most important element at the tightest point of the spiral — typically the eye of a face, a hand holding an object, or the subject's center of gravity.

**Emotional impact:** Harmonic beauty, classical weight, the sense that this composition has always been inevitable.

**Best use:** Hero shots, character reveals, moments of mythic or emotional significance.

**Prompt phrase:** `golden ratio composition, subject placed at spiral focal point, classical harmonics`

---

## Leading Lines

Lines in the environment (roads, shadows, walls, fences, corridors) that draw the eye toward the subject or through the frame.

**Variations:**
- **Converging lines** (train tracks, corridor) → depth, inevitability, destination
- **Diagonal lines** → dynamism, instability, energy
- **Horizontal lines** → calm, stability, stasis
- **Vertical lines** → power, solitude, confrontation

**Prompt phrase:** `leading lines, [environmental lines] direct eye toward subject, depth reinforced`

---

## Negative Space

The empty area of the frame surrounding the subject. The subject occupies less than the frame expects.

**Emotional impact:** Isolation, vulnerability, smallness, loneliness, the weight of the world pressing in. Alternatively: contemplation, openness, freedom — depending on what fills the negative space.

**Usage:**
- A small figure in a vast environment → insignificance, epic scale
- A character with empty space before them → potential, the unknown ahead
- A character surrounded by negative space → total isolation

**Prompt phrase:** `significant negative space, subject small against [environment], deliberate emptiness, [emotional register — isolation / freedom / contemplation]`

---

## Depth — Foreground, Midground, Background

A flat frame has one plane of information. A layered frame has three, each adding meaning.

**Layering principle:**
- **Foreground:** The element closest to camera. Creates visual texture and often connotes threat, proximity, or immediate danger. Something in the foreground unseen by characters signals what the audience knows that they don't.
- **Midground:** Primary subject of the shot lives here.
- **Background:** Context, world, atmosphere, scale. Sometimes: threat approaching.

**Prompt phrase:** `three-layer depth, foreground [element], subject in midground, background [environment/threat], depth field creates visual journey`

---

## Framing Within the Frame

Using environmental elements — a doorway, a window, trees, a crumbling arch — to create a secondary frame around the subject inside the frame.

**Emotional impact:** The subject is contained, watched, or isolated. Their world is defined by the frame that holds them.

**Usage:**
- Power: Character framed by a grand arch → mythic scale
- Imprisonment: Character framed by narrow walls or bars → trapped
- Voyeurism: Seen through a window or gap → surveillance, exposure
- Intimacy: Two characters sharing a natural frame → connection

**Prompt phrase:** `frame within a frame, subject seen through [doorway / arch / window / gap], natural environmental framing`

---

## Symmetry

The frame is divided into mirrored halves. Subject at center. Left and right are equal.

**Emotional impact:** Authority, confrontation, formality, control, the oppressive precision of order. Used in architecture, power structures, and moments of direct psychological confrontation.

**Best use:** Villain reveals, institutional settings, moments of deadlock between two equal forces.

**Prompt phrase:** `symmetrical composition, subject centered, equal left-right balance, formal geometry`

---

## Asymmetry

The frame is deliberately unbalanced. Subject and counterweight are mismatched.

**Emotional impact:** Tension, instability, something unresolved, movement potential. The image feels like it's about to become something else.

**Prompt phrase:** `asymmetrical composition, visual imbalance, tension in the frame, deliberate off-balance weighting`

---

## Silhouette

Subject is rendered as dark shape against a bright background. All detail lost. Shape and form only.

**Emotional impact:** Mystery, archetype, the figure as symbol rather than individual. Power of the unknown. One of the most visually memorable compositions in cinema.

**Usage:**
- Hero establishing shot — sunrise or sunset silhouette
- Villain reveal — the shape of threat before identity
- Final image — a character walking into or against the light

**Prompt phrase:** `silhouette composition, [subject] dark against bright [sky/fire/window/light], all detail removed, pure form`

---

## Eye Line and Look Space

Characters looking in a direction create a vector across the frame. The audience follows that vector.

**Look space rule:** If a character looks right, give them space on the right side of frame. Cutting off their look space creates discomfort.

**Breaking look space intentionally:** Used to create a feeling of being trapped, cornered, or denied — the character has nowhere to look.

**Prompt phrase:** `look space, subject at [left/right], looking toward [direction], space maintained in look direction`

---

## Headroom

The space between the top of the subject's head and the top of the frame.

- **Too much headroom:** Floating, unanchored, disoriented
- **Too little headroom:** Oppression, claustrophobia — used deliberately in confined scenes
- **Correct headroom:** A small deliberate gap, respecting proportion

**Prompt phrase:** `correct headroom, small space above subject, grounded in frame`

---

## Composition Checklist

For every shot:

```
[ ] Is there a clear primary subject?
[ ] What does the composition say about how we feel about that subject?
[ ] Is the rule of thirds / golden ratio applied deliberately?
[ ] Are leading lines present and pointing at the subject?
[ ] Is the depth of field creating appropriate layers?
[ ] Is negative space deliberate?
[ ] Is symmetry or asymmetry serving the scene's emotion?
[ ] Is the eyeline respected or broken intentionally?
[ ] Does the composition reinforce the dominant emotion of the shot?
```

## Composition Tables by Emotional Need

| Emotional Need | Composition Tool |
|---|---|
| Scale / epic grandeur | EWS + negative space + leading lines into distance |
| Intimacy | MCU + shallow focus + subject fills frame |
| Power | Low angle + centered symmetry + vertical lines |
| Vulnerability | High angle + negative space + headroom compressed |
| Isolation | Negative space + subject small + flat background |
| Confrontation | Symmetry + both subjects facing camera + even frame weight |
| Danger | Dutch angle + foreground obstruction + broken look space |
| Mystery | Silhouette + frame-within-frame + minimal information |
| Triumph | Low angle + centered + upward leading lines + sky in background |
| Defeat | High angle + negative space + compressed headroom |

---

## Golden Rule

> Every element in the frame is an argument. Composition is the editor of the static image — it tells the audience where to look, how to feel, and what to think before any action occurs.

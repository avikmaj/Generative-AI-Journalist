# Storytelling — Structure, Arc & Scene Design

## Why Structure Matters

Structure is not a formula — it is a map of human psychology. Audiences are wired to track: desire → obstacle → escalation → resolution. A story that follows that map is satisfying regardless of genre. A story that ignores it feels incomplete regardless of how impressive the visuals are.

Choose a structure before writing a single scene.

---

## Structure 1 — Three-Act Structure (default)

Best for: features, drama, action, crime, thriller, horror, war

```
ACT I — SETUP (25%)
  Ordinary world → Inciting incident → Protagonist commits to the goal

ACT II — CONFRONTATION (50%)
  Rising obstacles → Midpoint shift (all-in or false victory) → Dark night of the soul
  
ACT III — RESOLUTION (25%)
  Climax → Consequence → New equilibrium
```

**Scene design rules for Three-Act:**
- Every scene must change something. If nothing is different at the end of a scene, cut it.
- Escalate the stakes at every major beat. Each obstacle harder than the last.
- The dark night (late Act II) is the emotional lowest point — design it as the most visually and tonally oppressive moment in the film.
- The climax is not the action peak — it is the moment the protagonist's internal flaw and external goal converge.

---

## Structure 2 — Hero's Journey (Campbell / Vogler)

Best for: mythology, adventure, epic, fantasy, origin stories

```
1. Ordinary World
2. Call to Adventure
3. Refusal of the Call
4. Meeting the Mentor
5. Crossing the Threshold
6. Tests, Allies, Enemies
7. Approach to the Inmost Cave
8. Ordeal (near death / supreme test)
9. Reward
10. The Road Back
11. Resurrection (final test, transformation complete)
12. Return with Elixir
```

**Key cinematic moments to design:**
- **Crossing the Threshold** — the visual moment the protagonist enters a new world. Wide shot, environmental contrast.
- **Ordeal** — the lowest, most dangerous moment. Darkness, isolation, physical damage.
- **Resurrection** — the hero is reborn changed. Light returning, posture shifting, decisive action.

---

## Structure 3 — Dan Harmon Story Circle

Best for: episodic, genre-flexible, character-driven

```
1. A character is in a zone of comfort
2. But they want something
3. They enter an unfamiliar situation
4. Adapt to it
5. Get what they wanted
6. Pay a heavy price for it
7. Return to the familiar situation
8. Having changed
```

**The circle's strength:** The character at step 8 is measurably different from the character at step 1. Design visual storytelling to show that change — wardrobe, body language, environment reflecting the internal shift.

---

## Structure 4 — Save the Cat (Blake Snyder)

Best for: high-concept commercial, action, comedy

15 beats:

| Beat | Timing | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Opening image | 1% | Visual statement of the theme |
| Theme stated | 5% | Someone says what the film is about |
| Set-up | 1–10% | Protagonist and flawed world established |
| Catalyst | 10% | Life-changing event forces the story |
| Debate | 10–20% | Should I go? Should I act? |
| Break into Two | 20% | Protagonist commits |
| B Story | 22% | New character / relationship introduced |
| Fun and Games | 20–50% | The premise is explored |
| Midpoint | 50% | False victory or false defeat |
| Bad Guys Close In | 50–75% | Things get worse |
| All Is Lost | 75% | Rock bottom |
| Dark Night of the Soul | 75–80% | Internal processing |
| Break into Three | 80% | Solution discovered |
| Finale | 80–99% | Execute the solution |
| Final Image | 99–100% | Mirror of opening — what has changed? |

## Character Arc Design

A character without an internal arc is a plot device. An arc is the gap between who they are and who they need to become, closed — or deliberately not closed — over the course of the story.

### Arc Types

| Type | Description | Genre fit |
|---|---|---|
| Growth arc | Character moves toward better version of self | Drama, adventure |
| Fall arc | Character moves toward worse version of self | Tragedy, noir, crime |
| Flat arc | Character is already right; changes the world around them | Action hero, mythology |
| Disillusionment | Character loses a false belief | Thriller, war, crime |
| Corruption | Character gains a false belief | Villain origin, dark drama |

### Designing the Arc

```
INTERNAL FLAW: What false belief does the character hold at the start?
EXTERNAL GOAL: What do they consciously pursue?
INTERNAL NEED: What do they actually need (different from what they want)?
ARC TRIGGER: What event forces them to confront the flaw?
CRISIS CHOICE: The moment when they must choose between the lie and the truth
TRANSFORMATION: What has changed internally by the final scene?
```

### Visual storytelling of the arc

The character's internal state should be readable from the image — not told in dialogue. Use:
- **Posture** — collapsed at low points, upright at resolution
- **Proximity** — isolated or enclosed when afraid, open space when free
- **Wardrobe** — can shift subtly to signal internal change
- **Eye direction** — downward at shame, forward at resolve
- **Colour surrounding them** — cold tones at low points, warm at transformation

## Scene Design

Every scene is a mini-story. It has a beginning (status quo), middle (conflict), and end (new status quo). Apply this to even a 10-second clip.

### Scene Objective Framework

For every scene, define:

```
SCENE LOCATION + TIME:
DRAMATIC PURPOSE: (why does this scene exist in the larger story?)
CHARACTER GOAL: (what does the protagonist want IN THIS SCENE?)
OBSTACLE: (what prevents them from getting it?)
SUBTEXT: (what is really being said beneath the surface dialogue?)
EMOTIONAL ARC: (how does the dominant emotion shift across the scene?)
EXIT STATE: (how is the situation different at the end vs the start?)
```

### Scene Types

| Type | Function | Camera tendency |
|---|---|---|
| Establishing | Orient the audience to a new world or location | Wide, slow, environmental |
| Confrontation | Characters in direct opposition | Tight, cutting, unstable |
| Discovery | Character learns crucial information | Push-in, tight CU, breath held |
| Action / Chase | Physical conflict | Handheld, fast cut, wide-to-tight |
| Intimacy | Emotional connection between characters | Medium-to-CU, static or slow float |
| Turning point | The story changes direction permanently | Hold on face, minimal cut |
| Aftermath | Processing a major event | Wide, isolated, slow |

## Subtext

What characters say and what they mean are rarely the same thing. Subtext is the real meaning beneath the surface text.

**Rule:** Never write dialogue that says what a character is feeling. Show the feeling, say something else.

```
BAD: "I'm angry that you left."
GOOD: "The food got cold." [said quietly, eyes down, long pause]

BAD: "I'm afraid to do this."
GOOD: [character stares at the door. Puts their hand on the handle. Pulls it back. Puts it on again.]
```

Design subtext at the shot level too. The frame says things the script doesn't.

---

## Conflict Types

All story conflict traces to one or more of these:

| Type | Description | Cinematic expression |
|---|---|---|
| Person vs Person | Direct opposition | Confrontation scenes, fight choreography |
| Person vs Self | Internal war | CU on face, internal monologue, isolation |
| Person vs Nature | Environment as antagonist | Wide shots dwarfing character, weather FX |
| Person vs Society | System or institution as antagonist | Wide cold environments, power asymmetry in frame |
| Person vs Fate | Inevitability, prophecy | Recurring visual motifs, mirrors |
| Person vs Technology | Machine or system as threat | Cold light, geometric environments |

## Emotional Arc Design (scene-level)

Design the emotional journey of every scene as a curve:

```
ENTRY EMOTION → COMPLICATION → PEAK → RELEASE → EXIT EMOTION
```

Example (crime thriller interrogation scene):
```
Entry: Controlled, professional [neutral light, medium shots]
Complication: Suspect deflects [tighter cuts, slight push-in]
Peak: Investigator loses composure [extreme CU, static camera, silence]
Release: Suspect reveals something unexpected [wide pull-back, breath]
Exit: Investigator alone, recalculating [wide, isolation, dim]
```

Map this curve before designing a single shot.

## Dialogue Writing Principles

1. **Every line of dialogue serves one of:** character revelation, story advancement, or subtext. If it does none of these, cut it.
2. **People talk past each other.** Real conversations are not information exchanges — they are competing agendas. Write the agenda, not the information.
3. **The pause is dialogue.** Design silence as deliberately as words.
4. **Interruptions are power.** Who interrupts who reveals the power dynamic.
5. **Word choice reveals character.** A soldier speaks differently than a diplomat. Never let all characters sound like the writer.
6. **The last line of a scene is the exit beat.** It should close the immediate conflict while opening the larger one.

## World Building

A world is believable when its rules are consistent, even when those rules are impossible.

### World Building Framework

```
ERA / TIME PERIOD:
GEOGRAPHY: (climate, terrain, settlement patterns)
SOCIETY: (power structure, class system, factions)
CULTURE: (customs, rituals, values, taboos)
RELIGION / MYTHOLOGY: (belief systems, sacred objects, cosmology)
TECHNOLOGY / MAGIC: (what is possible, what is forbidden, what is rare)
HISTORY: (what events shaped this world into what it is now?)
ECONOMICS: (who has resources, who doesn't, how does that create conflict?)
AESTHETICS: (visual design language — architecture, fashion, colour, light)
SOUND WORLD: (what does this world sound like at rest?)
```

---

## Golden Rule

> Every story is a character who wants something badly and has difficulty getting it. Design the want. Design the difficulty. Everything else is execution.
